Arabic Live Captions for the Classroom: What the Evidence Shows

We tested our new Extreme Arabic engine on a 13-minute talk of fast, colloquial Arabic with English mixed in, and on 100 recordings with known transcripts. This page shows what it gets right, where it is better than YouTube's captions and our previous engine, where it still fails, and what we would check before relying on it for Arabic-speaking students.

Summary for decision makers

Where it is strong

  • 4.2% word error rate on 100 real Egyptian-Arabic recordings (previous engine: 19.6%).
  • On a 13-minute colloquial talk it agrees with YouTube's post-processed captions to within 14% of words, and with our previous engine only to within 32%.
  • It keeps English terms in English letters ("screen time", "every single day"), which both comparison systems garbled.
  • It adds punctuation, which makes live captions easier to read.
  • Runs on the device: no internet and no audio sent to a cloud.

What to know before relying on it

  • Captions appear a few seconds after the speaker pauses, not word by word.
  • Mac only, with the app language set to Arabic.
  • In one stretch of heavy English it failed (repeated a word); we have since added a guard against that.
  • Not yet tested in a real classroom: room echo, distant microphones, several speakers.
  • No speaker labels. A teacher should expect occasional errors on names and technical terms.

Test 2: a 13-minute colloquial talk

Method. We cut the audio at pauses into 43 segments (13.5 minutes in total) and ran the same segments through Extreme and the previous engine. We took YouTube's automatic Arabic captions for the same video, which YouTube produces after the fact with more context than a live system has.

The previous engine split "عم بيعطوا" into non-words and read 150 as 100. Extreme (like YouTube) got the number right.

Where it still fails

Repetition loop on heavy English (found in this test)

In one 17-second stretch where the speaker switches into English phrases, Extreme wrote one word, "ستاندرد" (standard), 33 times in a row. The other systems also struggled there (YouTube produced unrelated words, the previous engine produced English-looking nonsense), but a repeating word is the more disruptive error on a screen.

What we did: the app now cuts any word repeated more than three times in a row. This is a safety net against this one failure type, not a fix for the underlying difficulty with fast English inside Arabic.

Smaller issues

  • English words are sometimes written in English letters and sometimes in Arabic letters ("اتس فيري ديفيكالت") within the same talk.
  • Rare words and some proper names are wrong (for example "ات توبل" for a short English phrase).
  • A segment of background noise at the end of the talk produced the stray word "ضوضاء" (noise).
  • A few sentences end early or late at segment boundaries, because the engine reads each phrase after a pause.

Is it suitable for Arabic-speaking students?

QuestionAnswer from our tests
Is accuracy high enough to follow a lecture?On clean speech, yes (4.2% word error rate). On fast, informal talk the text matched YouTube's after-the-fact captions closely, and kept the meaning of the passages we examined. Not yet verified in a real classroom.
Are captions live?Yes, but phrase by phrase, a few seconds after the speaker pauses. A Release build transcribes a 12-second phrase in about half a second; most of the wait is for the pause.
Does it handle teachers who mix Arabic and English?Better than the comparison systems in our test, but heavy stretches of English remain the weakest point.
Which Arabic?Tested on Egyptian speech (recordings) and Levantine-flavoured colloquial speech (talk). Gulf, Maghrebi and Sudanese Arabic are untested. Modern Standard Arabic lectures are not yet measured separately.
Privacy?Recognition runs on the device. No audio is sent for transcription.
Equipment?A recent Apple-silicon Mac, a one-time model download of about 1.5 GB, and a close microphone.
What is not provided?Speaker labels, a human proofreader, and any guarantee for legal or exam accommodations.

Our recommendation for a pilot

  1. Run Extreme in two or three real classes with a lapel or desk microphone close to the teacher.
  2. Ask two Arabic-speaking students to mark errors that changed the meaning, and compare with the engine you use today.
  3. Keep a person ready to correct names and technical terms, especially in lessons with a lot of English.
  4. Treat live captions as an aid for understanding, and use the saved transcript with human review where accuracy is formally required.

What these tests do not show

  • No human reference transcript for the 13-minute talk; its figures are agreement between systems.
  • One speaker, one talk and one dataset. Different voices, dialects and rooms can change the results.
  • Word error rate counts every wrong, missing or extra word; it is not the same as how much meaning a student gets.
  • We have not tested a noisy classroom, several speakers, or a distant microphone.
