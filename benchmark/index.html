Neural Engine

Apple’s Neural Engine (ANE) is a low-power accelerator for machine-learning models. We checked whether each engine uses it and whether other processor choices would be faster.

Which chip runs the model

  • Basic and Standard: both stages of the model run on the Neural Engine by default.
  • Mix: the main model stage loads on CPU + Neural Engine; we confirmed this at load time.
  • Rapid: run by the operating system, so which chip it uses is not visible to the app.
  • Power, measured system-wide while the engines ran: the Neural Engine sat at 0 mW when idle and averaged roughly 50–70 mW (peaks under 1 W), against about 0.7–1.2 W for the CPU, so it carries only about 4–9% of the compute power.

A second test: a live press conference

A clean recording is the easy case, so we also ran a very different one: a NASA news conference with several speakers, technical vocabulary and unusual names. We streamed 2 minutes 53 seconds of it through the engines at real-time speed, exactly like the first test, and scored the captions against a corrected transcript.

Check it yourself. The audio is NASA’s Artemis II post-splashdown news conference (April 10, 2026). Open it at 6:38 (the link jumps there); the test runs from 6:38 to 9:31, from “They are Amit Kshatriya…” to “…I’ll hand it over to Dr. Glaze.” Press play on the video and watch our captions beside it.

Word error rate in detail

Word error rate is the share of the reference words that came out wrong: (wrong + missed + extra words) ÷ words in the reference. Lower is better, and 5% means about one word in twenty is off. The reference has 562 words (not counting filler sounds).

Filler sounds (“uh”, “um”) are ignored in the main score, as on the rest of this page. The small line under each engine is the stricter score that counts them too.

What the errors were

We sorted every counted error into what actually went wrong, so you can see whether a score reflects hard names and word endings or real mistakes.

Examples of the errors: “Amit Kshatriya” heard as “Amet Shatriya” or “Ahmed Shatriya”, “touched” written as “touch”, “packed” heard as “peck”. Names like these are the hardest part of any live captioning job, which is why we kept them in the score.

What counted as correct

  • Case, punctuation and number formatting (“25,000 ft” or “twenty-five thousand feet”).
  • The same word written differently: “Dr.” or “doctor”, “programs” or “programmes”, “we were” or “we’re”, “it” or “it’s”.
  • Words the speaker said twice by accident (“then then take questions”, “as soon as as soon as”): the engine may caption them once or twice.
  • A few stretches no listener can make out on the recording: two place names (“Michoud”, “Bremen”), a third in “Teams at Stennis”, the surname “Radigan”, one garbled phrase (“…in an incredible way”) and a speaker’s self-correction (“That is… that is a thousand people”). We marked those spots as wildcards in the reference, about 25 words in all.

What still counted

  • Every other wrong, missed or extra word, including all of the people’s names, whether or not an engine could be expected to know them.
  • Filler sounds count only in the stricter score under each bar.
  • The reference started from the video’s automatic captions and was corrected by hand against the recording.

The honest summary

  • Mix was the most accurate here at 2.8% (16 errors in 562 words); Rapid was close behind at 4.8% (27 errors) and showed captions about 25× sooner on average (0.05 s against 1.24 s).
  • 7 of Mix’s 16 errors and 9 of Rapid’s 27 were names or word-ending differences; the rest were similar-sounding words and short dropped or swapped words.
  • One clip, one Mac, English, a clean broadcast feed. Room noise, distant microphones and heavy accents will move every number, and we have not compared other captioning products on this clip, only our own engines.

How to read this

What was measured

  • Two minutes of continuous, natural English speech, played at real-time speed in 100 ms chunks.
  • Each engine runs the same segmentation and decoding logic the app uses when listening live.
  • Models are downloaded and warmed up first, as in the app’s setup screen; load time is reported separately.

What it does not tell you

  • One clip and one machine. Repeating the same engine varies by about 1–2 points of WER and a few tenths of a second, so treat small gaps as a tie.
  • Clean studio audio. Room noise, distant microphones and accents will move every number.
  • English only. Other languages are not covered here.

Choosing an engine

  • Rapid: fastest captions and lightest on memory; needs iOS or macOS 26.
  • Mix: top accuracy, works on older systems, slightly later captions.
  • Standard / Basic: well under half of Mix’s peak memory, for lower-memory devices; Standard is the more accurate of the two.
