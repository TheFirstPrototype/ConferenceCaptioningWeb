Add Live Translated Audio to Wireless Conference Headsets (No Internet Required)

Saamer Mansoor•November 7, 2026

To add live translated audio to wireless conference headsets: start ConferenceCaptioning on a Mac, open the interpreter page, choose a language and a voice, and send the computer's audio output to a spare channel on your headset transmitter. Attendees switch to that channel on their headset and hear the translation about one second after each sentence ends (Rapid mode). Everything runs on the device, so you need no internet in the hall and no interpreter booth.

Why add translation channels to wireless headsets?

Silent-seminar-style headset systems already solve the hard part of listening in a busy hall: every attendee wears a headset and picks their own channel, and the systems on the market offer many of them. For example, the makers of the conference headsets at Silent Seminars describe 10 and 45 channel systems with programmable, lockable channels. What each extra channel needs is an audio source in another language. Booking human interpreters for every language and every session is expensive, and often not possible. ConferenceCaptioning turns a computer into that source: it listens to the room, translates, and speaks the translation, on a channel of its own.

What you get

  • The translation read aloud, sentence by sentence, with a voice you choose, including the Premium voices that ship with macOS.
  • Your choice of voice: pick the gender, the voice and the accent your listeners hear for each language, or turn on automatic gender detection so the voice follows who is speaking when two people share the stage.
  • Test tones for the left, right and both sides, and a "speak sample" button, so your AV team can check every cable and channel before doors open.
  • Setup instructions on the page itself for Dante Virtual Soundcard, USB interfaces and a second computer.
  • No internet in the hall. The page, the translation and the voices all run on your side of the room.
  • On-screen captions at the same time. The same app shows live captions and translations on your screens, which headsets cannot do for attendees who are deaf or hard of hearing.

How it works

A microphone or the mixer feeds the app. The app captions the speech, translates each finished sentence, and the interpreter page reads the translation aloud through the computer's sound output. That output goes to your transmitter, which sends it to the headsets as one channel. The page runs in a web browser on the computer next to the transmitter (or on any computer on the same network), and the app serves it at http://{device IP}:8080/interpreter.html.

Set it up in six steps

Step 1: Prepare, once, with internet

  1. Install the app and download the translation languages you will use.
  2. Install a good voice for each language: on a Mac, System Settings > Accessibility > Spoken Content > System Voice > Manage Voices, and download a Premium or Enhanced voice.
  3. After installing a voice, fully quit your browser (Cmd+Q) and reopen it. Browsers only list new voices after a restart. Online-only voices will not work in a hall with no internet, so the page never picks them on its own.

Step 2: Start captioning

Connect your audio source (mixer, audio interface, Dante or a microphone), choose the speaking language and the language to translate to, and start captioning in Rapid or Mix.

Step 3: Open the interpreter page

  1. In the app, tap the Local pages button (the network icon) and open the interpreter card, or open http://{device IP}:8080/interpreter.html in a browser.
  2. Choose the language, pick a voice, and press Start speaking. Browsers require one click before they play sound.

Step 4: Send the audio to a channel of its own

The page speaks through the computer's current sound output, and a web page cannot choose the output or a single channel by itself, so you do the routing:

  • Dante Virtual Soundcard: set the output to Dante Virtual Soundcard and subscribe only TX channel 2 to the transmitter's channel 2 input. English stays on channel 1 from the mixer.
  • USB interface or headphone jack: use a stereo-to-two-mono splitter and connect only the right leg to the transmitter's channel 2 input.
  • Another computer on the same network: open the same address on it and wire its output the same way. This is also how you add a second language.

Step 5: Test the whole chain

Press Tone: left, Tone: right and Tone: both, and Speak sample with this voice, then listen on a headset tuned to the channel. Do this before the event, with the transmitter powered and the headsets in range.

Step 6: Turn on "Match the speaker" (optional)

Tick Match the speaker, then choose a male voice and a female voice for the language. Each sentence is now read in the voice for whoever was speaking when it was said. The page shows what it hears, for example Speaker sounds female (about 243 Hz). Leave "keep the voice used last" ticked: it stops the voice changing when the app is not sure.

Choose the voice, or let the app match the speaker

For each language you choose the gender, the voice and the accent your listeners hear, for example a British or a Canadian French voice. If one person speaks, that is all you need. If two people share the stage, turn on automatic gender detection and give the page a male and a female voice.

Hearing a translation in a voice that matches the person on stage makes a conversation easier to follow, especially in a panel or a Q&A. The app measures the pitch of the microphone audio, which separates most male voices (typically about 85 to 155 Hz) from most female voices (about 165 to 255 Hz), and the interpreter page picks the voice you chose for that sound. Nothing is shown to the audience, no audio is stored, and you can switch it off.

It is a hint, not a fact. Pitch alone cannot separate every voice: a low-pitched woman or a high-pitched man sits in a zone where the app says "not sure" and keeps the voice it used last. Please read the numbers below before you rely on it for an event with many speakers.

How fast is it, step by step?

After a speaker finishes a sentence, the translated voice starts about one second later in Rapid mode. This is where that second goes, measured on a Mac with the demo script:

StepTime
The speech recognizer decides the sentence is finishedabout 0.6 s (0.3 to 1.0 s)
The app notices, translates (0.03 s) and hands the text to the voice pageabout 0.2 s
The voice starts speakingabout 0.1 s
Total when the voice is freeabout 0.9 s

The biggest piece is the recognizer, which waits for a short pause before it will call a sentence final. We used to wait for the next sentence to start before speaking, which added about two seconds; the app now speaks a sentence as soon as the recognizer finalizes it. A sentence only waits longer when the voice is still reading the one before it, which happened to about a third of the sentences in our demo script and added about a second each.

What to know before the event

  • It is machine translation. It is very good on everyday conference language, but idioms can come out wrong: in our own tests "Exactly right" was translated as the French for "exactly to the right". Run your key phrases through it first, and keep human interpreters for legal, medical or other high-stakes sessions.
  • Fast talkers. A voice cannot read faster than people comfortably listen. If the speaker talks continuously and quickly, the translation falls behind. In one 16-minute test with continuous speech, half of the sentences waited more than 4.6 seconds because earlier sentences were still being read. The page then speaks up to 30% faster and skips ahead past about 300 characters to catch up. For a fast speaker, ask them to leave short pauses.
  • One language per page. A web page cannot choose where its sound goes, so each language needs its own computer, or a browser routed to its own audio device.
  • Overlapping speakers are not handled: when two people talk at once the voice choice is a guess.
  • Your headset system is your own. We have not tested every brand of transmitter. Anything that takes an audio input and sends it to a channel should work, and the tone buttons tell you quickly.

Pro Tips

  1. Keep the computer awake (System Settings > Lock Screen, or run caffeinate in Terminal) and keep the interpreter tab open and in front of nothing that mutes it.
  2. Keep the address from changing: give the computer a static IP so the page address works from one event to the next. Here's how on a Mac.
  3. Speed: if sentences pile up, the page speaks a little faster and skips ahead past about 300 characters, so the headsets stay close to live.
  4. Mission-critical show? Add a backup device with hot-failover redundancy.
  5. Put captions on the screens too. Headsets help people who hear; screens help everyone, including deaf and hard-of-hearing attendees.

Frequently asked questions

Does this work with silent seminar and wireless conference headsets?

Yes, in principle. The app produces audio from a computer, and any wireless-headset system with a spare audio input on its transmitter can carry it as a channel of its own. Attendees switch channels on the headset as they always do. We have not tested every brand of transmitter, so test your own chain with the tone buttons before the event.

Do I need internet in the hall?

No. Download the translation languages and install the voices once at the hotel, and everything else runs on the device. The interpreter page works over your local network. The computer needs internet only for that one-time setup.

How long after a sentence ends does the translated voice start?

About one second after a sentence ends in Rapid mode, when the voice is free: roughly 0.6 seconds for the speech recognizer to decide the sentence is finished, 0.2 seconds for the app to translate it and hand it over, and 0.1 seconds for the voice to start. On our demo script the voice was called 1.1 seconds after the end of a sentence on average. Mix mode, which we measured earlier, took about 2.8 seconds. If the speaker talks faster than the voice can read, the voice falls behind: it then speaks a little faster and skips ahead rather than drifting further.

How many languages can I send at once?

One language per interpreter page, because a web page cannot choose which audio output it plays through. For several languages, use one computer per language, each wired to its own transmitter channel. Routing several browsers on one computer to different audio devices with a sound-routing tool may work, but we have not tested it.

How does the automatic male and female voice work?

The app measures the pitch of the microphone audio, which tells it whether the speaker sounds male or female, and the interpreter page reads each sentence in the male or female voice you chose. It is a hint, not a fact: in our tests on 66 real speakers it was right about 96% of the time for one speaker at a time, and 81% to 84% of sentences got the right voice when people took turns every few seconds. When it is unsure it keeps the voice used last, so the voice does not flip back and forth while one person talks. No audio is stored.

How accurate and how fast are the captions that feed the translation? We measure them in the open, on a real NASA news conference, and publish the method, the data and the scoring script. Read the live captioning accuracy and delay benchmark.
